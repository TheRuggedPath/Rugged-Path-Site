Justin Klahn: When Control Ends, Surrender Begins

Justin Klahn has worked alongside some of the world’s most highly regarded innovators, creators, and elite operators—experiences captured in his Innovators Handbook. But this conversation isn’t about his work or résumé.

It’s about God.

When the relationship with his young daughter’s mother ended—suddenly, from Justin’s perspective—he opted to live out of his truck for the next three years rather than take work that would steal any time away from her. He chose to prioritize every hour he could with his daughter between the ages of three and six.

During those lean years, he was literally living on God’s daily bread. A close friend encouraged him to try praying with gratitude.
Justin said he had none to offer—until one night, during a walking prayer, he surrendered everything to God and finally broke through.

Justin and Dan spotlight division among churches, discuss a global death penalty for crimes against children, and are moved by a woman raised within generations of Luciferian belief who was called out of darkness by Christ later in life.

A testimony of fatherhood, failures, forgiveness, and blazing trails for God.

The Story

Justin Klahn built a career most people would call impossible to walk away from. Twenty years inside Nike Innovation, embedded with JSOC units building gear that had to actually work in the field, consulting with intelligence agencies, DARPA, and Harvard's Learning Innovations Laboratory, and a run working directly for Kanye West. He is a civilian. He has never served. But decades of proximity to that world, and a career built on solving problems other people couldn't, gave him a kind of access and credibility few civilians ever have.

About three years after his own first real encounter with faith, his relationship with his daughter’s mother ended. Justin is direct that this was his own doing, not his family's. He had prioritized his mission, first his career, then years of counter child-trafficking work, over the home he was supposed to be building. What followed was not immediate change but years of reckoning: a season of sleeping in his truck, living on little, so he could be fully present for his young daughter rather than working. A men's group he started called Fight Club for Christ, civilians and veterans bringing their real struggles to the circle instead of polished testimonies.

"I'm not grateful. And I'm not going to give up this hate and anger."

A friend named Prime told him, bluntly, that it didn't seem like he'd actually surrendered to God. It stung enough to sit with him for two more years. During that season, on a hillside trail in San Marcos, California, where Justin would walk for hours at a stretch carrying a kettlebell and praying, a disturbing and frightening thought about ending his own life passed through his mind and was gone as quickly as it came. He calls it the only time in his life he's come close to something like that, and a glimpse into a darkness he'd never understood in other people until he felt it himself.

Two years into the separation of his family, a close friend could tell Justin was having a hard day and challenged him to try something simple: a gratitude prayer. Justin tried. He found he genuinely couldn't. "I'm not grateful," he said. "And I'm not going to give up this hate and anger." That moment of honesty, more than any resolution, is where real change actually started.

Eventually, on a night praying barefoot on a California beach, Justin prayed for liberation from spiritual deception, for himself and for the people he was in conflict with, his daughter's mother and her mother. He experienced a joy and release he says he'd never felt before and has never fully left him since. The next morning, a years-long conflict with his daughter's grandmother ended without warning, a text out of nowhere offering support he never expected. Justin also spent years in counter child-trafficking work that brought him face to face with what he describes as real spiritual warfare, work that cost him something every time he talks about it.
